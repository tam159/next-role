The Dossier

A recruiter knows things about their client companies that never reach a job description: what Acme actually runs, what they are staffing next month, how the last two AI projects went. That knowledge is real, it is scattered across seven systems, and it is the only input that makes tailoring measurably better. This is how to turn it into a knowledge base an agent can use — and what to build first.

Six decisions, up front

Architecture

Not one KB — four stores

Documents, compiled facts, relations and live records change at different rates and need different engines. Forcing them into one index is the classic failure.

The core question

LLM wiki and chunk index, layered

The OKF wiki is the product surface — reviewable, citable, demoable. The hybrid chunk index is the evidence substrate underneath it. Not either/or.

Latency

Move agentic search to compile time

NextRole's queries are predictable per company and per role. Do the expensive multi-hop reasoning once, nightly, not on the user's request.

Freshness

Webhooks first, cron as the net

Push where the API allows it, cursor-poll where it doesn't, and a nightly reconcile diff to catch deletes and missed events. Split the cheap reindex from the expensive recompile.

Buy vs build

Borrow the plumbing, build the compiler

Onyx gives away 50+ connectors with permission sync; CocoIndex gives away incremental recompute with lineage. Nothing open source compiles company dossiers — that is the part worth your time.

Before any of it

Prove the uplift on 20 hand-written pages

The whole thesis — more internal context yields better resumes and prep — is untested. Two weeks of manual curation answers it. Build the pipeline after.

01

The knowledge in play

Not a knowledge base so much as a compiler: it turns a recruitment company's scattered internal material into grounded, citable facts an agent can tailor against.

The client already owns the knowledge. What they don't own is the ability to use it: it sits in a five-year-old Confluence space, in a Notion database one team migrated to, in Slack threads, in Meet transcripts nobody reopens, in Drive PDFs named Acme_TechReview_v3_FINAL.pdf, and in an ATS whose free-text notes fields are where the real intelligence lives. Asking their employees to follow a convention has already failed, and it is not going to start working now. The convention has to be imposed after the fact, by a compiler, not before the fact, by policy.

What a fact looks like once it is compiled

Your example, made concrete. Three source systems, one claim, one provenance chain:

ClaimWhere it came fromConfidence signal
Acme builds agents on Python + LangGraph Confluence "Platform overview", rev 41 · Meet transcript 2026-08-14 @ 11:40 two independent sources, 9 days old
Project Atlas kicks off 2026-09, needs 3 backend + 1 ML ATS req AC-2291 · exec deck slide 12 one source is leadership-only — see §09
Two prior agent projects; the first stalled on eval tooling Retro doc in Drive · Slack thread in #acme-delivery 14 months old — decay this
They dropped Airflow for Temporal in July Recruiter's own correction, typed into the dossier human, newest, must survive recompile

Those four rows are what changes a resume from "5 years Python, built AI features" into "built LangGraph agent pipelines; set up the eval harness that the team was missing" — and what turns interview prep from generic STAR drilling into "they will ask how you'd migrate Airflow DAGs to Temporal."

Two audiences, one artifact

Worth designing for from the start: the dossier is useful to the recruitment company's own consultants, not just to candidates. “Which of our clients run LangGraph and are hiring in Q4” is a question they ask every week and currently answer from memory. That is a cross-entity query, which is why L3 exists at all — and it means the same compiled layer has to serve two very different read patterns.

The constraint that shapes the data model. The recruitment company's counterparties are the hiring companies. Some of what is in that Confluence space is under NDA and cannot be repeated to a candidate, even though it would help them. Every fact therefore needs a disclosure tier from day one — candidate_safe, recruiter_only, client_confidential — filtered at generation time, not bolted on later. Retrofitting this into a compiled corpus is a rewrite.

02

Complex KB, or just an LLM wiki?

The question assumes they compete. They don't — they sit at different layers, and the mistake is picking one.

Sort the client's knowledge by how it behaves, not by where it lives. Three kinds fall out, and each one wants a different engine:

KindExampleVolumeChange rateEngine
Documents Confluence pages, Drive PDFs, Slack threads, Meet transcripts 10⁴–10⁶ chunks constant, low-value churn hybrid chunk indexBM25 + dense + rerank
Facts "Acme runs LangGraph"; "Atlas starts in September"; "eval tooling was the gap" 10²–10⁴ pages slow, but contradicts itself across sources compiled wikiOKF markdown, one page per entity
Records ATS requisitions, pipeline stages, placements, warehouse tables 10⁵–10⁸ rows every minute query it livesemantic layer → SQL, never embedded

Read that table twice, because it settles three arguments at once.

  • Documents cannot be the answer surface. Retrieval over raw chunks rediscovers the same synthesis on every query, at query-time latency, with query-time failure modes. The recruiter asks about Acme forty times a week and pays the reasoning cost forty times.
  • Facts cannot be a vector index. The value here is reconciled knowledge — the exec deck and the retro doc disagree, and something has to decide. That is synthesis, and it belongs in a durable artifact. It also has to be reviewable: a recruiter can read a markdown page about their own client and tell you it's wrong. Nobody can review an embedding.
  • Records must never be embedded. A snapshot of the ATS in a vector store is stale the moment it lands, and lossy on top. Grounding an LLM in a modelled semantic layer instead of raw schemas is reported at roughly 83% vs 40% natural-language query accuracy in dbt's own testing — vendor numbers, so treat the gap as directional, not exact. Either way: tools, not chunks.

The verdict. Karpathy's wiki pattern is the right product surface — the thing the agent reads first and the client reviews. A hybrid chunk index is the right evidence substrate — what the wiki cites, and where the agent falls back when the wiki is thin. Google's OKF is the right file format for the wiki, and its v0.2 trust fields (provenance, trust, freshness, lifecycle, attestation) are almost exactly the metadata this problem needs. You already run an OKF bundle for docs/prd/; the muscle exists.

03

The layers, specified

Four read paths plus a landing zone and one compile-time artifact. Every pick is open source and self-hostable, and where possible already running in NextRole.

LayerHoldsTechnologyWhy this one
L0 · Landing Original bytes plus the parsed DoclingDocument JSON, keyed by content hash SeaweedFS + obstore Already in the stack. Content addressing gives you free dedup, a replayable pipeline, and a traceable delete path for GDPR erasure.
L1 · Chunk index Structure-aware chunks with contextual prefixes, embeddings, BM25 terms, the access-control list (ACL) inherited from the source, source revision Postgres + pgvector + ParadeDB pg_search One database, one transaction, both retrieval modes. Postgres-resident vector search holds to roughly the low tens of millions of vectors — far past any single recruiter's corpus. Move to Qdrant only if a client blows past that.
L1½ · Fact atoms One row per atomic claim, with its citations, validity interval and the narrowest ACL among its sources Postgres table, written by the compiler A compile-time artifact, not a fifth read path — which is why it is absent from the layer figure. It is what makes citation, contradiction detection, ACL floors and erasure operate at claim granularity instead of page granularity. See below.
L2 · Dossier One OKF markdown page per company, project, technology, team and role Markdown in Postgres + object store; compiled by a LangGraph pipeline Reviewable, diffable, greppable, portable. The format is a spec, not a vendor. It is also the only layer a non-engineer can read, check and correct.
L3 · Relations Typed edges: uses, staffs, succeeded, migrated_from, with validity intervals Postgres tables + recursive CTE Derive edges from L2 frontmatter and links — do not run a second LLM extraction pass over raw text. A graph database earns its place only when the eval set actually contains 3+ hop questions.
L4 · Live records Nothing. It is a tool, not a store. Cube (OSS core) over BigQuery / Snowflake / Databricks; the ATS's own REST API Governed metrics the agent queries over MCP instead of writing raw SQL against tables it has never seen. Warehouse-native semantic views are a fallback if the client already models there.

Why there is a layer between chunks and pages

Compiling straight from chunks to a finished page is the obvious design and it is the wrong one. Everything you need to do afterwards — cite a claim, notice two sources disagreeing, work out the narrowest ACL, honour an erasure request, decide whether a recompile is even necessary — happens at the granularity of a claim, not a page. So extract claims first, as rows:

atom_id        a1f9c2…                      # stable hash of (subject, predicate, object)
subject        org:acme-robotics
predicate      uses_technology
object         tech:temporal
valid_from     2026-07-01                  # when it became true in the world
valid_to       null                        # open interval = currently true
observed_at    2026-08-18                  # when we learned it
citations      [conf://…/88213#L41, human:2026-08-18]
acl_floor      recruiters_all              # min() over every citation's ACL
disclosure     candidate_safe
conflicts      [a77b31…]                   # the Airflow atom this one supersedes
confidence     0.92                        # corroboration count × source trust × recency

Two independent designs landed on the same shape, which is some comfort: Tencent's agent memory distils raw material into “atoms” before it builds scenario and persona pages, and Semantica makes conflict detection a step that runs before a merge rather than a cleanup afterwards. Note the two time fields. That is the bi-temporal idea from Graphiti, applied to a table you already know how to query — a superseded atom is invalidated with a valid_to, never deleted, so “what did we believe in June” stays answerable and the audit trail survives.

Three details in there that are load-bearing. Footnotes are non-negotiable — an uncited fact in this system is a fabrication risk with a candidate's name on it. And acl_class is derived, not declared: a page inherits the narrowest permission of anything it cites, or it leaks (§09).

04

Ingestion

Borrow the connector tier. Build the compiler tier. The differentiation is entirely in the second one.

Connectors: the honest build-vs-borrow

Seven source systems with permission sync is six months of unglamorous work, and none of it is your product. Three credible paths, and the answer is a blend of the first two:

PathWhat you getWhat it costs
Onyx as the connector + chunk tierMIT community edition, ~32k stars 50+ connectors — Confluence, Notion, Slack, Drive, SharePoint, Jira, Discord — with permission sync that reflects source ACL changes within minutes. Hybrid keyword + semantic index. REST API and MCP so your agent can query it as a tool. You inherit a whole product: its Postgres, its index, its model servers, its chunking opinions, and a community/enterprise edition split to watch. Deep customisation means forking.
CocoIndex as the incremental engineApache-2.0, Rust core + Python DSL, ~11k stars You declare target = f(source) and it keeps the target in sync. Per-row lineage plus memoisation on hash(input) + hash(code) means only the delta reprocesses — including when you change the transform itself. Sources cover Drive, S3, filesystems, databases and message queues; targets cover Postgres and pgvector, Qdrant, Neo4j and Kuzu. Retries, backoff and dead-letter queues come with the Rust core. You adopt its dataflow abstraction, and it has an enterprise tier — the same split to watch as Onyx. It gives you no permission sync: for Confluence, Notion, Slack and Discord you still need a connector that mirrors ACLs.
dlt pipelines you ownPython, incremental state built in A thin extractor per source landing raw payloads into L0. Native to your stack — Python 3.13, uv, runs in the same container as everything else. You own the freshness semantics exactly. Roughly one to two weeks per source, and you rebuild both permission sync and incremental invalidation yourself, badly, twice.

Recommendation: Onyx reaches the sources, CocoIndex moves the deltas, you write the compiler. Spike Onyx for a week and let it own the systems it already covers, consumed as a retrieval tool over its API. Run the L0→L1 lane on CocoIndex rather than hand-rolled watermarks — its code-aware invalidation is precisely the primitive this architecture needs, and rebuilding it yourself is the classic six-month detour. Keep dlt for the handful of sources neither reaches: the client's own ATS app and Meet transcripts. Build L1½, L2 and L3 yourself, because no open-source project compiles entity dossiers with provenance, disclosure tiers and an ACL floor, so that part has to be yours either way.

Parsing and chunking

Docling is an easy yes and there is no real competitor: MIT, hosted by LF AI & Data, ~65k stars, and it covers the whole spread — PDF layout and reading order, DOCX/PPTX/XLSX, HTML, EPUB, OCR for scans, plus ASR for audio and keyframes for video, all normalised into one DoclingDocument. Its HybridChunker respects document structure instead of cutting at 512 tokens, which matters more than any embedding choice.

On top of it, apply contextual retrieval: prepend a 50–100 token LLM-written description of where each chunk sits in its parent document before you embed and before you index it for BM25. Anthropic's published numbers on their own corpus: top-20 retrieval failure drops from 5.7% → 3.7% with contextual embeddings, to 2.9% with contextual BM25 as well, and to 1.9% once a reranker is added. It costs an LLM call per chunk at ingest and nothing at query time — exactly the trade this architecture wants.

Docling or PageIndex? Both, and not for the same job

These are not alternatives, and reading them as a choice is the most common way to get this wrong. Docling turns bytes into a document. Everything goes through it, always — it is the only thing that knows how to read a PDF's columns, a DOCX's styles or a WAV file. PageIndex turns one long document into a navigable tree. It runs after Docling, on the minority of documents where splitting destroys the structure that carries the meaning, and it runs at compile time only.

What arrivesDocling's jobThen what
Confluence / Notion pagestructured HTML, a few thousand words Normalise to DoclingDocument; real heading tree; tables kept as tables, not as flattened text Docling only. HybridChunker splits on the headings the author already wrote. A tree index would add nothing you don't have.
Meeting transcript or Slack threadWebVTT, MP3, chat JSON ASR for audio; speaker turns and timecodes preserved Docling only, with a transcript-shaped chunker: split on speaker turn plus a time window, never on token count. Keep the speaker — “the CTO said” and “a contractor said” are different claims.
Short PDF or deckunder ~20 pages Layout, reading order, table structure, figure captions via VLM Docling only. The whole thing fits in a prompt; a tree is overhead.
Long PDF80-page playbook, org handbook, RFP, client account review Same parse — Docling still does the reading Docling → PageIndex. Build a tree over the natural sections with an LLM summary per node, then let the compiler ask “which section covers their deployment model?” and walk to that subtree. Chunk-and-embed on an 80-pager retrieves paragraphs that are locally similar and globally wrong.
Scanned or image-heavy PDF OCR plus the VLM pipeline — this is where Docling earns its keep Docling only. PageIndex's open-source build targets text-heavy PDFs; scans need their cloud, so keep them out of the tree path.
XLSX / CSV Extract the grid faithfully Neither chunker. A sheet is records. Extract atoms from it, or route it to L4 and query it. Chunking a spreadsheet is a category error.

Pick the long-document threshold once and write it down: roughly 20 pages or 15k tokens, which is also OpenKB's own default for switching to a tree. Below it, read the document whole; above it, index and navigate. In a recruiter's corpus this will route something like 5% of documents to PageIndex and 95% straight to chunks — but that 5% is the account reviews and playbooks, which is where the highest-value facts live.

Entity resolution — the hidden hard problem

"Acme", "Acme Inc.", "ACME Corp", "acme.com" and ATS account 7741 must collapse to one dossier, or the KB fragments and every retrieval misses. This is not an LLM problem, it is a record-linkage problem, and it should be cheap and auditable:

  1. Canonical key is the primary domain. Not the name. Names collide and mutate; domains mostly don't.
  2. Deterministic normalisation — case, legal suffixes, whitespace, punctuation — then block on normalised name plus domain.
  3. Splink for probabilistic linkage on the ATS and CRM tables. Open source, Fellegi–Sunter, runs on a SQL backend, gives you inspectable match weights instead of a vibe.
  4. LLM adjudication only on the ambiguous middle — the 2–5% Splink scores between thresholds. Log every decision; a wrong merge silently corrupts two dossiers.

Apply the same discipline to technology names. langgraph, LangGraph, "langchain graph" and "LG" are one node; LangChain, Langfuse and LangSmith are three others. Curate that alias table by hand — it is a few hundred rows and it is worth more than a bigger embedding model.

Worked example: one page, three questions

One real Confluence page all the way from webhook to dossier, then three questions that use it. This is the section to show someone else.

Everything above is architecture. Here it is running. The source is a single Confluence page in the client's SPACE-ACME space — “Acme platform overview”, about 2 400 words with one table and one architecture diagram — which an Acme-side engineer has just edited to say the team moved off Airflow.

Which engine owns which lane

Yes, install CocoIndex — but only for the cheap lane. The two engines are not competing for the same work, and the split follows from the shape of each job:

LaneShape of the workEngine
L0 → L1the four numbered pills above Per row, order-independent, idempotent, no conversation. Run it a thousand times and you want the same answer and no duplicated work. CocoIndex. This is precisely a memoised dataflow. Nothing about it wants a state machine.
L1 → L1½ → L2 → L3the two teal arrows Multi-step with branches: adjudicate a conflict or don't, retry a synthesis whose citations failed, give up and escalate to a human. Carries state across steps. LangGraph. That is a graph with conditional edges, you already run it, and the compiler is the part with no off-the-shelf equivalent.

They meet at one table. CocoIndex owns the chunk rows; when it re-derives a chunk, a trigger writes the affected (entity, page) pairs into a dirty_pages table. LangGraph drains that table on a debounce. Neither engine knows anything about the other, which is the point — you can replace either one without touching the other.

One trap worth flagging before you read a tutorial. CocoIndex v1 replaced its whole API. flow_def, FlowBuilder, add_source, collect/export and the cocoindex setup step are gone, and nearly every blog post and example you will find online still uses them. v1 is @coco.fn functions, coco.App, and mount/mount_each. Read the docs, not the tutorials.

The flow for one source, in v1 shape. Note where the incremental behaviour actually comes from — it is the one decorator argument:

"""L0 → L1 for one source. Run: cocoindex update flows/confluence.py -L"""
import cocoindex as coco
from cocoindex.connectors import postgres

PG      = coco.ContextKey[asyncpg.Pool]("pg")
EMBED   = coco.ContextKey[SentenceTransformerEmbedder]("embedder")

@dataclass
class ChunkRow:                          # this dataclass IS the L1 schema
    chunk_id: str
    source_ref: str                        # conf://SPACE-ACME/page/88213
    text: str                              # context prefix + body
    embedding: Annotated[NDArray, EMBED]   # dims inferred from the ContextKey
    allowed_principals: list[str]          # mirrored from the space ACL

@coco.fn(memo=True)                       # ← the whole reason to use this library
async def process_page(page: StagedPage, table: postgres.TableTarget[ChunkRow]) -> None:
    doc    = await docling_parse(page.blob_sha)          # ① from L0 bytes
    chunks = hybrid_chunker.chunk(doc)                       # ①
    await coco.map(process_chunk, chunks, page, table)     # ②③④ per chunk

@coco.fn
async def app_main() -> None:
    table = await postgres.mount_table_target(
        PG, table_name="l1_chunks",
        table_schema=await postgres.TableSchema.from_class(ChunkRow, primary_key=["chunk_id"]),
    )
    table.declare_vector_index(column="embedding")
    src = postgres.PgTableSource(coco.use_context(PG), table_name="l0_staged_pages",
                                row_type=StagedPage)
    await coco.mount_each(process_page, src.fetch_rows().items(lambda p: p.source_ref), table)

app = coco.App(coco.AppConfig(name="ConfluenceToL1"), app_main)

That memo=True is doing four things you would otherwise write yourself. The cache key is the arguments plus a hash of the function body, so editing process_chunk — or swapping BGE-M3 for Qwen3 — invalidates exactly the rows that depended on the old code and leaves the rest alone. Rows whose source content is unchanged are skipped. Primary keys make the writes targeted upserts. And a row deleted from l0_staged_pages is deleted from l1_chunks on the next pass, which is half of the tombstone requirement in §06 handled for free. It does not retract the atoms that chunk supported — that lives in the LangGraph lane and is still yours to build.

If you decide not to take the dependency, the minimum you must hand-roll is two tables: source_state(ref, revision, content_hash, seen_at) and derivation(output_pk, input_hashes[], code_hash). “What is stale” then becomes a join, and a prompt edit becomes a code_hash bump. Write those two tables before you write anything else in the lane — retrofitting lineage onto a pipeline that already has a million rows in it is the worst week of the project.

Fair challenge, because “fetch by entity id” quietly skips a step. Two things it is not: the agent does not carry a roster of dossier names in its context, and it does not read an index file. Resolution is a database index, tried in order, first hit wins:

Total resolve-and-fetch is on the order of 10–25 ms. Which raises the obvious point about that 0.3 s label: every tier latency in this brief is time-to-assembled-context, not time-to-answer. Generating a tailored resume is a long completion and dwarfs all of it. Two rules fall out of the ladder. Rung ③ must return a confidence, and a weak match is a miss — resolving “the robotics company” to the wrong Acme silently tailors a resume against another company's stack, which is worse than any retrieval miss because it looks confident. Rung ④ is a feature, not a failure: a question with no resolvable entity is a tier-2 question, and handing off is the correct behaviour. And note the alias table is not new work — it is the same table §04 builds during entity resolution at ingest, read back at query time.

06

Staying fresh when everything changes

Push where the API allows it, poll where it doesn't, reconcile nightly because both of those will fail silently.

The single most useful idea in this section: separate the cheap update from the expensive one. Reindexing a changed chunk costs an embedding call and a row upsert — do it on every event, immediately. Recompiling a dossier page costs a multi-step LLM synthesis over a dozen sources — do it in batches, debounced, and only for pages whose cited evidence actually moved. Conflating the two is how teams end up either stale or bankrupt.

TierTriggerTarget lagCatchesFails at
A · PushSource webhook or gateway eventseconds edits, creates, most deletes silent webhook drops; expired Drive watch channels; sources with no webhook
B · PollCursor on updated_at or a change token 15 min hot / 1 h cold everything Tier A misses on covered sources hard deletes, permission changes, anything that doesn't bump the cursor
C · ReconcileNightly cron, full listing diff24 h deletes, ACL changes, missed events, expired watches, drifted revisions nothing — this is the backstop, and skipping it is why enterprise KBs rot
RecompileDirty-page set, debounced15 min – 1 h synthesis catching up with new evidence needs an on-demand override, or a recruiter who just fixed something waits an hour to see it

Three rules that keep it from rotting

  • Deletes are a compliance path, not a cleanup task. A removed Confluence page must purge its chunks and retract the facts it supported. The chunk half comes free if the cheap lane runs on CocoIndex — a row that disappears from the source is deleted from the target on the next pass. The second half is yours: when an atom loses its last citation it is closed, the page's trust drops, and the claim is flagged for review rather than silently kept.
  • Bound the recompile cost by citation, not by time. Only recompile pages whose cited refs changed. A 2,000-page corpus where 40 sources move a day should be tens of recompiles, not two thousand.
  • Your own code is a source. The invalidation nobody plans for: when you change the compiler prompt, the chunker or the extraction schema, every page built by the old version is stale even though no document moved. Key the dirty set on hash(citations) + hash(compiler_version) and a prompt edit re-derives exactly the affected pages instead of forcing a full rebuild — or leaving a corpus quietly compiled by four different prompt versions. This is the primitive CocoIndex provides natively, and the main reason to run the ingest lane on it rather than on hand-rolled watermarks.
  • Run the lint as a scheduled job. Karpathy's third operation is the one everyone drops: sweep weekly for contradictions between pages, claims past their ttl_days, orphan pages nothing links to, and single-sourced claims presented as settled. Output a queue for a human, not an auto-fix.
07

Retrieval

Three tiers with three latency budgets, and one structural move that makes agentic search affordable.

Agentic depth genuinely buys accuracy — corrective grading, decomposition and reflection all move the numbers — but the reported cost is roughly 2–5× latency and 3–10× tokens, which no interactive surface can absorb. The way out is not to skip it. It is to notice that NextRole's queries are predictable: the questions are per company and per role, and you know the company and the role before the user asks. So run the expensive reasoning at compile time, once, and let the request path be a lookup.

Why BM25 is a correctness requirement here, not an optimisation

This domain runs on rare proper nouns. LangGraph, LangChain, Langfuse and LangSmith sit almost on top of each other in embedding space and mean completely different things about a company's stack. Same for Temporal vs Airflow, or a project codename that appears in four documents and nowhere else on the internet. Dense retrieval blurs exactly the tokens this product is built to get right — and a resume that claims stack alignment on a hallucinated framework is worse than a generic resume, because it fails visibly, in front of the hiring manager. Exact lexical matching is load-bearing. Do not ship a dense-only configuration.

Model picks

embedding
BGE-M3 (MIT) as the default — battle-tested, 100+ languages, and it emits dense, sparse and multi-vector representations from one model, which simplifies the hybrid arm. Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B/4B (Apache-2.0) if you want the stronger instruction-aware option and can afford the serving.
reranker
bge-reranker-v2-m3 is the quality-per-millisecond default — roughly 71–72% BEIR nDCG@10 at around 12 ms per pair on a mid-range GPU. Qwen3-Reranker-8B leads the open-weight board near 77% if the accuracy is worth roughly 3× the latency. Numbers here come from 2026 aggregator round-ups, not first-party evals — verify before you commit.
long docs
PageIndex for the 20+ page minority — org handbooks, RFPs, client decks — where chunking destroys the structure that carries the meaning. Run it at compile time to pull the right sections into a dossier, never on the request path: its retrieval is an LLM walking a tree, which is the opposite of a latency budget.
graph
Postgres tables with a recursive CTE until the eval set proves you need more. Revisit only if 3+ hop questions show up for real; then Graphiti is the interesting option, because its bi-temporal model (valid time vs. ingest time, invalidate rather than delete) is a genuinely good fit for "Acme was on Airflow, is on Temporal".
skip
Microsoft GraphRAG's global search — dozens of LLM calls per query and a reported $0.50–2.00 per complex query is the wrong shape for this. If you want graph-flavoured retrieval cheaply, LightRAG is the pragmatic cousin at roughly one call per query, with correspondingly weaker whole-graph aggregation.
08

Measuring it, or you are guessing

One gold set, four metrics, and one of them is a fabrication counter that can block a release.

Build the gold set with the client's own consultants, before the pipeline. 150 rows is enough: a question, the company it is about, the facts a correct answer must contain, and the source that proves each one. Getting recruiters to sit down and write those 150 rows is also the cheapest possible discovery exercise — you will learn what they actually ask, which is almost never what you assumed.

MetricWhat it catchesToolingGate
Context recallThe fact was in the KB and retrieval missed it Ragas, wired into LangSmithno regression vs. the last release
Context precisionRetrieval returned noise that dilutes the prompt Ragaswatch, don't gate — precision trades against recall
FaithfulnessThe answer drifted from the retrieved context Ragas / LLM judgeno regression
Fabrication rate A claim about a company that no source supports. The one that does real damage. custom check: every factual claim must resolve to a citation in L1 or L2 hard zero on the gold set. Blocks release.
Staleness rateAnswers built on facts past their ttl_days frontmatter scan, no LLM neededreport weekly
Disclosure violationsA recruiter_only fact reaching candidate outputtag propagation test over generated artefacts hard zero. Blocks release.

Two practical notes. LLM-judge metrics have real run-to-run variance, so compare across at least three runs before you believe a two-point move — and pin the judge model, because changing it silently rebases every historical number. And measure the thing you are actually building: run a blind A/B where consultants rate resumes and prep briefs produced with the dossier against ones produced from the job description alone. That comparison, not recall@20, is the one that tells you whether any of this worked.

09

Access and disclosure

Non-negotiable for anyone holding recruitment data. Synthesis also has an access-control failure mode that chunk retrieval does not.

Start from the easy win: deploy single-tenant per client. A per-client deployment is the operating model anyway, and it removes the entire class of cross-tenant leakage bugs that would otherwise dominate your security review. Inside the tenant, mirror source permissions onto every chunk at ingest — an allowed_principals array — and pre-filter both retrieval arms on it. If the client's rules are relational rather than list-shaped ("the account team for this client, plus their manager"), put OpenFGA or SpiceDB in front and pre-filter on the authorised id set; over-fetch and post-filter only as a second pass, never as the only pass.

Now the part that is specific to this architecture, and easy to miss:

The rest of the governance checklist

  • Disclosure is orthogonal to ACL. A recruiter may be permitted to read a fact and still be contractually barred from repeating it to a candidate. Two independent fields, both enforced: acl_class gates retrieval, disclosure gates generation.
  • Candidate PII never enters a dossier. CVs and candidate notes live in their own store with their own lifecycle. A company dossier that mentions a named candidate is a bug, and the fabrication check should flag it.
  • Exclude DMs and private channels outright. Employee chatter is personal data even inside a work tool. Allow-list channels; never wildcard.
  • Erasure has to reach every layer. A deletion request must sweep L0 bytes, L1 chunks, L2 citations and L3 edges. Content-addressed landing plus footnote-level citation is what makes that traceable rather than aspirational.
  • Log every read. Which principal saw which dossier, when. An HR company will require it, and retrofitting audit logging is miserable.
  • Name the provenance fields after a standard, not after yourself. The provenance block above is ad-hoc; W3C PROV-O is the published vocabulary for exactly this (wasDerivedFrom, wasGeneratedBy, wasAttributedTo). Mapping to it costs a rename now and buys you a compliance reviewer who recognises the shape of your audit export. Semantica models its lineage this way, which is the strongest argument in its favour even if you never install it.
10

Build order

Each phase has an exit criterion. If a phase fails its criterion, the next one is not worth building.

PhaseBuildExit criterion
0 · Prove it~2 weeks, no pipeline One client, one Confluence export, 20 companies. Hand-write 20 dossier pages in the OKF schema. Point the existing career agent at the folder as files. Run the blind A/B against job-description-only output. Consultants measurably prefer the dossier-grounded output. If the blind A/B comes back flat the premise does not hold, and no amount of pipeline fixes it — two weeks spent, not six months.
1 · The spine Docling parsing on a CocoIndex flow for two or three sources, L0 landing, L1 with pgvector + pg_search and contextual prefixes, the L2 compiler as a LangGraph pipeline, Tier 1 and Tier 2 retrieval, and the gold-set harness from day one. Context recall on the gold set beats the Phase-0 manual pages. Fabrication rate is zero. A recruiter can read a generated dossier and say “yes, that's Acme”.
2 · Make it live Webhook tier, cursor-poll tier, nightly reconcile, debounced recompile, tombstones, ACL mirroring and pre-filter, entity resolution with Splink, the weekly lint job. A page edited in Confluence is reflected in candidate output within an hour, without anyone running a command. Deletes verifiably disappear.
3 · Breadth and depth Onyx for the long tail of connectors, L3 edges and Tier-3 agentic search, Cube over the warehouse, ATS tools, PageIndex for long documents, per-visibility-class compiles. A second client onboards in under two weeks of engineering time. Until that holds, this is a bespoke integration rather than a product.

What not to build yet

  • A general enterprise search product. That is Onyx, it is MIT, and it is three years ahead of you.
  • A graph database. Not until the gold set contains 3+ hop questions that Postgres recursive CTEs actually fail on.
  • Fine-tuned embeddings. Contextual prefixes plus a reranker will beat a fine-tune for a fraction of the effort, and they don't need retraining when the corpus changes.
  • Multi-tenant isolation. Single-tenant deploys until you have enough clients that deployment cost hurts more than the isolation work.
  • Everything in Slack. Allow-listed channels only. Compile cost scales with volume; value does not.

Questions only the client can answer

Worth asking early, because three of these change the architecture.

  1. What may a candidate be told? Get the disclosure tiers in writing before you compile anything. This is the one that changes the data model.
  2. Where does the deployment live? Their VPC, your cloud, or on-prem. It decides whether you can use hosted models at all, and therefore whether self-hosted embeddings and rerankers are optional or mandatory.
  3. Which ten Confluence spaces and which ten Slack channels actually matter? The answer is always far smaller than "all of them", and it halves phase 1.
  4. Who owns a wrong dossier page? Named reviewers per client account, or the trust: reviewed state never gets used and the whole quality story collapses.
  5. Does their ATS have a read API, or are we doing CDC off its database? Changes phase 1 scope by a week either way.
  6. How stale is too stale? Their answer sets ttl_days and therefore the recompile bill.
